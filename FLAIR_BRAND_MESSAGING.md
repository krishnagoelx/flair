# Flair brand messaging

Working foundation for the U.S. developer website, 14 September 2026. This is messaging strategy, not a statement that every proposed product capability has shipped. The preferred brand line remains **“Give your AI a flair for shopping.”**

## 01. Core message

**Flair helps consumer AI apps turn user intent into completed shopping by finding the right products and deals, executing purchases, and carrying the order back into the app.**

The host app owns the relationship and understands the user. Flair supplies the commerce intelligence and execution. The difference from a product feed, affiliate link, or checkout button is the complete, context-aware shopping task.

## 02. Value proposition

Flair gives consumer AI developers a way to make shopping happen inside their apps. It uses the context their AI already has to select relevant, purchasable products, find a worthwhile deal, complete an authorized order, and return its status to the app. The shopper gets something they actually need; the partner can earn from the completed sale without building the entire shopping stack.

## 03. Tagline decision

| Line | Style | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Give your AI a flair for shopping.** | Witty, ownable | **Recommended brand payoff.** It uses the name naturally and is memorable. It needs a direct product headline beside it because it does not explain the mechanism alone. |
| The shopping capability for your consumer app. | Functional | Best first-impression descriptor and leading homepage headline. The category and audience are immediately clear. |
| Your AI can finish the shopping. | Functional | Strong purchase-execution emphasis. Less complete on finding products and post-purchase. |
| When intent appears, shopping happens. | Aspirational | Captures the in-app moment, but is abstract without a product demonstration. |
| From recommendation to order. | Functional | Short and legible as a section line; undersells product choice, deals, and partner earnings. |

Use the functional descriptor to explain Flair at the top of the page and the approved witty line at the end. These are complementary roles, not competing public taglines.

## 04. Messaging hierarchy

### Level 1: brand headline

**The shopping capability for your consumer app.**

### Level 2: supporting statement

Your AI understands what the user needs. Flair finds the right product and deal, completes the purchase, and brings the order back into your app.

Primary action: **Book a demo.**

### Level 3: proof pillars

1. **Turn a user need into the right product.** Flair uses the moment, preferences, budget, and delivery constraints to make a shopping decision, not just return a catalog. Show the health coach recommending one exact protein variant, with a human-readable reason.
2. **Let your AI complete the purchase.** Flair checks the final offer and places an authorized order, then returns a real confirmation and status to the app. Show approval for the exact order or a clearly bounded standing purchase rule; never imply that all purchases happen without permission.
3. **Make commerce feel native to your app.** The experience can take the form the moment needs, from a confident single recommendation to a shortlist or bundle. Show the hairstylist’s product, deal, and presentation as one in-app experience rather than an external marketplace detour.
4. **Earn when your users buy.** The partner can see an attributed order and its earnings state. Show one order flowing into a partner earnings view, distinguishing estimated from settled earnings when appropriate.

### Level 4: proof bank

The website should use these only at the level actually demonstrated or verified before publication.

| Proof item | What the site can show | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One contextual recommendation | Health-coach screen connects user preference and goal to an exact product | Valid as an illustrative product flow; mark as demo if not live |
| Product, variant, and offer comparison | Price, availability, delivery, and deal considered together | Product-design requirement; validate operational scope |
| Authorized purchase | Exact-order confirmation or bounded purchase mandate, then merchant order confirmation | Product intent confirmed; validate shipped route |
| Order returned to partner app | Order ID, state, arrival information, and updates | Proposed integration contract; validate live events |
| Native shopping surface | Embedded/hosted experience, with headless and agent-tool paths as needed | Proposed integration modes; verify availability |
| Partner earnings | Attributed sale and estimated or settled commission | Proposed economics and dashboard; verify terms |
| Scale figures | 100 million products and 20,000 brands | User-supplied planning figures; verify U.S. scope, freshness, and purchasability before publishing |
| Marketplace breadth | Merchant and marketplace sources across categories | Do not publish “all marketplaces” until coverage is precisely defined |

## 05. Audience-specific messaging

| Audience | What matters | Lead with | Strongest proof | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer AI founder or product lead | Useful new capability, user experience, conversion, revenue | “Turn a moment of user intent into a completed order inside your app.” | One continuous shopper-to-order-to-earnings demo | Leading with SDKs, MCP, or catalog size |
| Developer or engineering lead | Integration surface, permissions, reliability, data boundaries, callbacks | “Pass Flair the relevant context and render a native shopping experience; receive authoritative order events back.” | Honest integration diagram, test flow, event states, security boundaries | “One prompt,” fictional API availability, unexplained black-box checkout |
| End shopper, reached through a partner app | Relevance, price, control, delivery, help after purchase | “Your assistant found the right one and can get it for you.” | Exact product, final total, delivery, authorization and order confirmation | Commission language, developer terms, covert automated spending |
| Merchants and brands, secondary company audience | Qualified demand, clean attribution, reliable execution | “Flair brings product demand from useful AI moments to purchasable offers.” | Verified order route and attribution, only when available | Implying every brand is a direct partner |

The homepage primarily serves the first two audiences. Shopper-facing language belongs inside the demo UI, not as the main page headline.

## 07. Things not to say

- Do not call Flair merely an MCP, SDK, widget, affiliate link, product database, or checkout API. Those may be access paths or components, not the customer outcome.
- Do not say “all marketplaces,” “every product,” “best deal,” “one prompt,” or “fully autonomous” without a bounded, verifiable meaning.
- Do not present 100 million products and 20,000 brands as purchasable U.S. inventory until the exact claim is substantiated. Catalog coverage, offer availability, and executable checkout are different.
- Do not equate a recommendation, an approved purchase attempt, and a confirmed order. The UI must show their distinct states.
- Do not say the AI buys without consent. Say it can complete purchases within a shopper’s explicit order approval or standing rules.
- Do not use aspirational pilots, partner names, merchant integrations, user counts, agent mailbox, or cross-app memory as current proof without release and permission checks.
- Avoid generic “agentic commerce layer,” “revolutionizing shopping,” “seamless experiences,” “monetize your AI,” and technical acronyms in the hero.
- Avoid em dashes in public copy, per the brand preference.

## Message test for every section

Can a U.S. consumer AI developer answer, in five seconds: **What is Flair? What does it do for my user? Does it actually buy? What do I have to integrate? Why should I care?** If a section does not help answer one of these, it should be cut or moved deeper into the site.
